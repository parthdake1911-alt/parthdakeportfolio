Project overview

Improving how the homepage communicates and guides

A homepage has to tell visitors who a company is, what it offers and where to go next. For an industrial gases company, that means getting complex services across quickly to very different visitors: plant buyers, hospitals, partners and investors.

I reviewed a screenshot of the existing homepage, identified where it could communicate more clearly, and turned those observations into a redesigned homepage built as an interactive HTML prototype.

The challenge

Making the homepage easier to understand and navigate

I looked at the existing homepage through six questions, answering only what the screenshot could show.

01

Information hierarchy

Is there a clear order between the main message, key content and the next step?

02

Navigation and discoverability

Are the main destinations easy to identify, and do labels say where they lead?

03

Homepage messaging

Does the first screen say who Ellenbarrie is and what the site is for?

04

Calls to action

Are the important next steps clear, and is emphasis given to the right ones?

05

Content structure and scanning

Is content grouped logically, and can a visitor scan it without effort?

06

Visual consistency

Do type, spacing, imagery and components support a clear, professional experience?

I only had a desktop screenshot of the current site, so I didn’t audit its mobile experience. My redesign prototype was also designed for desktop.

The UX audit

Identifying opportunities before redesigning

Five observations from the current homepage, each linked to what the redesign does about it.

The current homepage, annotated. Scroll inside the frame to see it all.

1

Top of the page

Opportunity
Observation
Above the navigation sits a dark bar with an email address, three phone numbers, social links and a welcome line. Below it are the logo, five menu items (Home, About Us, Our Business, Investors, Career), search and a red Contact Us button.
UX implication
Many small items of similar weight compete for attention before the visitor reaches any content. The same phone numbers appear again in the About section and the footer.
Opportunity
Give contact one clear, prominent route rather than many repeated ones.
Redesign response
The redesign puts a single Contact Us button in the hero. My prototype covers the homepage content only, so the navigation bar itself isn’t redesigned here.
2

The hero

Needs improvement
Observation
In the screenshot, the hero is an aerial photo of a gas plant with one centred “Our Services” button, and no headline.
UX implication
The first screen doesn’t say who Ellenbarrie is or what it does; a visitor has to scroll to find out.
Opportunity
Lead with a short positioning statement and one clear next step.
Redesign response
A headline, “India’s Trusted Industrial Gas Partner”, with a Contact Us button.
3

The About section

Needs improvement
Observation
The strongest facts (over 50 years of legacy, the largest 100% Indian-owned firm by capacity, revenue and profitability in Fiscal 2024, India’s largest oxygen plants at 1250 TPD) sit inside one dense paragraph of small grey text.
UX implication
Visitors who scan rather than read are likely to miss the company’s best proof points.
Opportunity
Pull the key facts out of the paragraph and make them visible at a glance.
Redesign response
A “Why Ellenbarrie?” section with four large figures in place of the paragraph.
4

The services

Needs improvement
Observation
Six service cards are staggered left and right around a central photo. Each has an icon, a small grey label and the same “Read More” link.
UX implication
The zigzag layout has no obvious reading order, the labels are small and low in contrast, and six identical links don’t help visitors tell the services apart.
Opportunity
Present the services as one clear, scannable sequence with names that stand out.
Redesign response
“What can we help you with?”: the services in one row, each with a bold name, a one-line description and an illustration.
5

Customers and footer

Positive observation
Observation
A row of six customer logos, and a footer with the registered office, CIN and contact details.
UX implication
These are useful trust signals for a B2B and infrastructure audience.
Opportunity
Keep them. They are worth carrying into any redesign.
Redesign response
My prototype focuses on the hero, services and proof points, so the customers section and footer aren’t part of it. A full build would keep them.
Redesign strategy

Moving from observations to design decisions

01

Lead with a clear message

Say who Ellenbarrie is in the first screen, in one line.

02

Simplify the first impression

One message and one action in the hero, instead of many small competing items.

03

A purposeful content flow

Who we are → what we can help with → why trust us, in that order.

04

Turn facts into proof points

Lift the strongest figures out of paragraphs and show them large.

05

A consistent visual system

One typeface, bold headings, high contrast, and red used only for emphasis.

I didn’t redesign the main navigation in this pitch; my prototype concentrates on the homepage content.

Key design decisions

Five changes, and why I made them

01 Hero02 Services03 Proof points04 Calls to action05 Visual system
01Hero and first impression

Say who Ellenbarrie is before anything else

Original

An aerial plant photo with a single “Our Services” button and no headline.

Observation

The first screen shows a facility but not what the company is or does.

Decision

I added the headline “India’s Trusted Industrial Gas Partner” and a Contact Us button over a refinery image.

Rationale

A one-line positioning statement answers the visitor’s first question straight away. A dark gradient behind the text keeps the headline readable over the photo.

02Services and content structure

From six scattered cards to one story about what Ellenbarrie does

Original

Six cards staggered around a photo, each with a small grey label and a “Read More” link.

Observation

No clear reading order, and names too small to compare at a glance.

Decision

I framed the section as a question, “What can we help you with?”, and showed the services in one row with names, short descriptions and illustrations, revealed as the visitor scrolls.

Rationale

One row reads left to right in a fixed order, and each service gets a plain one-line description, for example “Cylinders and Bulk tankers”.

  • A scroll-led sequence: an Ellenbarrie tanker drives across large “OUR SERVICES” type, then the line “We bring the gas to your gate.” introduces the six services. It literally shows what the company does.
  • One service in focus at a time: as the visitor scrolls, each service brightens in turn while the others dim. The six services are industrial gas supply, medical gases, medical equipment, gas pipeline services, cryogenic technology plants and EPC solutions.
  • Trade-off: in the prototype the services aren’t links yet. A full build would connect each one to its detail page.
03Proof points and hierarchy

Turning a paragraph into four figures you can’t miss

Original

“Welcome To Ellenbarrie Industrial Gases Limited” and one long paragraph of small grey text.

Observation

The most persuasive facts are buried mid-paragraph.

Decision

I replaced it with “Why Ellenbarrie?”, the line “Mission critical operations depend on reliable partners.” and four figures that count up as they appear: 50+, 1000+, 10+, 12+.

Rationale

Large figures can be read in a second. The headline answers why a buyer should trust the company, not just who it is.

04Calls to action

One clear next step instead of many equal ones

Original

Contact Us, Our Services and More About Us as red pill buttons, plus a phone link and six identical “Read More” links.

Observation

Many actions at the same visual weight make it unclear which one matters most.

Decision

I gave the hero a single Contact Us button and let scrolling carry the visitor through the rest of the page.

Rationale

For an industrial supplier, getting in touch is the most important action, so it gets the one prominent button.

10visible calls to action in the current homepage screenshot, not counting the top bar, menu and footer
1call to action in the redesigned prototype: Contact Us in the hero
05Visual design and readability

Bolder type, more contrast, and red only where it matters

Original

Small grey body text, faint watermark words (“About”, “Services”) behind headings, and pill buttons with a “+” on most actions.

Observation

Low-contrast text and decorative layers make the page harder to read and scan.

Decision

I used one typeface (Inter), large bold headings, solid red, black and white blocks, and dark text on light backgrounds.

Rationale

Higher contrast and a clear type scale make headings easy to spot and text easier to read, while keeping Ellenbarrie’s red.

From audit to prototype

Turning UX observations into a tangible proposal

Review

I examined the current homepage’s structure, messaging, hierarchy and navigation from a full-page screenshot.

Identify

I picked the observations that most affect clarity and could be tied to a concrete change.

Redesign

I redesigned the hero, services and proof points around those observations.

Prototype

I built the redesign as an interactive HTML prototype, with its scroll animations, so it could be reviewed as a real page.

Design outcome

A proposed homepage grounded in UX observations

The outcome is a redesigned homepage prototype that connects a focused audit to specific design decisions, so the current page and the proposal can be compared directly. This was a pitch: the prototype is a proposal, and it hasn’t been approved, launched or measured.

Deliverables

  • Homepage UX audit: five observations on the current homepage
  • Homepage redesign: a hero, services and proof points shaped by the audit
  • Interactive HTML prototype: the redesign with its scroll animations, for presentation and review

Reflection

This project reinforced that a redesign should start with the current experience, not a blank canvas. Tying each change to something I could point to on the current page made the pitch a reasoned UX proposal rather than a visual refresh. Next, I’d carry the redesign through the navigation, customers section, footer and mobile layout.
