Project overview

Translating a premium brand ambition into a digital experience.

The client already had a website but wanted a new experience that communicated a more luxurious and premium identity. That was an opportunity to rethink how the brand presented itself online, from its first impression to the way content and imagery were organised.

Rather than treating the redesign as a visual refresh, we looked at how established luxury brands build a distinctive digital presence. We studied websites such as Jaguar and Balenciaga for their approach to visual storytelling, typography, imagery, whitespace, composition and brand presentation, and used what we learned to shape the direction of the proposed ICA homepage.

The objective was to translate the client’s ambition for a more premium digital presence into a considered and cohesive homepage experience.

The design challenge

Making the digital experience feel as premium as the brand intended.

The project began with a clear requirement: a website that looked and felt more luxurious than the existing one. The challenge was turning the broad idea of luxury into specific, intentional design decisions.

  1. 01

    Defining what premium means digitally

    A premium experience isn’t created by adding dark colours, large images or elegant type. It depends on how those elements work together to communicate quality and brand character.

  2. 02

    Finding the right design references

    We needed to look beyond the client’s own industry to see how established luxury brands express their positioning through digital design.

  3. 03

    Translating inspiration into a distinct direction

    The goal was to find principles worth adapting to ICA, not to copy another brand’s website.

  4. 04

    Balancing visual impact with usability

    A striking homepage still needs a clear hierarchy, understandable content, useful navigation and purposeful calls to action.

Design opportunityDevelop a homepage that combines a more sophisticated visual identity with a coherent, usable website experience.

Synthesis: the principles that made it into the homepage

Five principles from the exploration are clearly visible in the redesigned homepage. Each one is grounded in what the prototype actually does.

01

Let imagery lead

Most sections are built around a full-screen image, with copy kept to a short caption.

02

Restrained typography

Light-weight Inter for headlines and body, small uppercase labels, and a serif kept for numerals.

03

One idea per screen

Each section fills the viewport, and on desktop one scroll gesture moves exactly one section.

04

Material as the story

Sand, oak, driftwood, stone, marble and brass carry the brand, alongside finished furniture shot as sculpture.

05

Quiet, precise motion

Words rise in, images unmask, and gold appears only when something responds to the visitor.

Visual hierarchy

Each screen has one image, one headline and at most one button. The image sets the mood, the headline names the idea and the button offers the next step.

Typography

Large headlines in a light weight read calm rather than loud. Scale and spacing carry the hierarchy, not bold weights.

Imagery and art direction

Macro textures and carefully lit furniture make the finish itself the subject. Several sections are almost entirely image.

Layout and whitespace

Full-height sections with text anchored to the left edge give the page a slow, deliberate rhythm. The white Highlights section breaks it on purpose.

Brand consistency

One type family, one button style, one accent colour and one counter style repeat across the page, the menu, search and footer.

Key design decisions

Six decisions, from client requirement to prototype.

Each decision traces back to the brief and forward to something you can see in the prototype.

01 First impression02 Visual storytelling03 Typography04 Layout05 Clarity06 Consistency
01
Design decision

Creating a stronger first impression

Client requirement
The homepage should feel luxurious from the first moment.
Decision
Open on a full-screen, three-slide hero preceded by a short branded loader, with the message reduced to a single caption.
Rationale
Leading with craft, material and product, rather than stacked banners and copy, lets the visuals set the tone before any text asks for attention.
Evidence in the prototype
The loader shows the ICA logo, “Italian wood finishes · since 1964” and a serif counter. The hero then rotates every seven seconds between a presenter (with a play control that opens a video), a sand texture and a sculptural cabinet. A ring on the next arrow shows the time to the next slide, line markers show position, and the caption reads “Italian Luxury wood finishes that bring even the most impossible ideas to life”.
02
Design decision

Using visual storytelling

Client requirement
Communicate a more luxurious brand character.
Decision
Tell the story through materials and styled objects: macro textures, finished furniture presented like sculpture, and a mosaic of named samples.
Rationale
ICA sells finishes, which are tactile. Showing texture at close range and products in gallery-like settings conveys craft better than describing it. This is where the reference study’s focus on art direction and storytelling shows most clearly.
Evidence in the prototype
“Now trending in Milan, and your living room” pairs a fluted plum cabinet with a classical bust. The Gallery section is almost entirely a sand texture. The Textures mosaic tags its tiles Brushed Brass, Driftwood, Raw Stone, Aged Oak · Ebony and Nero Marble, leading into the 3D Texture Library and mood boards.
03
Design decision

Refining typography and hierarchy

Client requirement
A more sophisticated presentation.
Decision
One restrained type system: light-weight Inter at large sizes, short two-line headlines, one supporting sentence and a small uppercase button, with Tinos serif reserved for numerals and the menu tagline.
Rationale
Hierarchy comes from scale and spacing rather than heavy weights, which keeps the page calm. Repeating the same headline–sentence–button pattern makes every section quick to read. The serif adds an editorial accent without competing with the headlines.
Evidence in the prototype
“You Imagine It, / We finish It.” and “Every finish / across the board.” follow the same pattern, each with one sentence and one button. Counters (“01 / 10”), menu numbers and “Shaping spaces. Elevating experiences.” are set in Tinos.

Serif is used sparingly: counters, menu numbers, the loader and this one tagline.

04
Design decision

Creating a more considered layout

Client requirement
A website that feels premium all the way down, not just at the top.
Decision
Compose the page as ten full-height sections, paced like a presentation, with one pinned section and one deliberate change of background.
Rationale
Giving each idea its own screen creates generous whitespace and a slower, more deliberate rhythm. Pinning the Expertise section lets four audience routes be explored without losing context. Switching to white for Highlights signals a change from brand storytelling to content.
Evidence in the prototype
On desktop, one wheel or arrow-key gesture moves exactly one section, and a pager shows the section name and “01 / 10”. In Expertise, the headline and presenter stay fixed while a rail of cards (Architect & Interior Designers, Experience Centres, Contractors & Applicators, Homeowners & Consultations) moves with a “01 / 04” counter.
05
Design decision

Maintaining clarity within a premium aesthetic

Client requirement
Premium, but still a website people can use.
Decision
Keep the header minimal (logo, search, menu) and put structure in full-screen overlays, then end the page with plain, explicit next steps.
Rationale
A sparse header protects the imagery, but only works if what sits behind it is well organised. Grouping the site into numbered categories, surfacing popular searches and ending with clear actions keep the experience usable.
Evidence in the prototype
The menu groups the site into six numbered sections (About ICA, Inspiration, Products, Services, Resources, Contact Us) that expand in place. Search opens with popular suggestions such as Finezza PU, 3D Texture Library and Experience Centres. The header hides while scrolling down and returns on scrolling up. The page ends with “Design My Dream Home”, “Discover Experience Center” and “Contact Us for In-Person Meeting”, plus an enquiry form with validation.
06
Design decision

Establishing visual consistency

Client requirement
A cohesive, sophisticated brand experience.
Decision
Use a small, strict set of shared elements: one button style, one gold accent reserved for interaction, one counter style and one motion vocabulary.
Rationale
Luxury depends on restraint. When the accent only appears in response to the visitor, it feels intentional rather than decorative, and repeated details make the page feel like one product.
Evidence in the prototype
Gold (#B89262) is used for the reading-progress bar, the hover fill on dark and sand buttons, the active form-field underline and label, and text selection. On desktop, a custom cursor becomes a labelled disc (“View”, “Read”, “Play”, “Visit”). Headlines rise in word by word, images unmask as they enter, and the prototype respects the reduced-motion setting.
Across screen sizes

The direction holds up on a phone.

Below 860px the prototype switches to stacked layouts with text over imagery and native scrolling. The custom cursor and section-by-section scrolling switch off, while the hero gains swipe navigation.

There is no before-and-after comparison here. I don’t have material from the original homepage to show, so rather than reconstruct it from memory, this case study focuses on the brief, the research and the redesigned outcome.

Reflection
Luxury is a design decision, not just a visual style.

This project showed me how much matters behind a visual reference. Studying established luxury brands pushed the exploration towards composition, art direction, typography and storytelling, rather than surface styling alone.

The hard part was turning those observations into a homepage that suits ICA, balancing the client’s wish for a premium aesthetic with the need for a clear, usable experience. It reinforced the value of tying visual research to specific, intentional design decisions.
