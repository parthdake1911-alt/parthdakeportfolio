Project overview

Making trading data easier to understand

Trading involves more than placing trades. Understanding your trading also means keeping records, reviewing past decisions and spotting patterns in how you perform.

Anchor explores how a dedicated desktop experience could bring that information into one organised place. In the prototype, a trader can:

  • Connect a broker with read-only access and import their trades, or continue with a journal-only account.
  • See their trading at a glance on a dashboard, then drill into any single trade.
  • Review each trade against a price chart, its executions and the rules of the strategy it was tagged with.
  • Keep a daily journal with notes and reusable templates.
  • Analyse performance by day and time, strategy, symbol, risk, tags, and wins versus losses.
  • Define strategies as rules, so Anchor can check every trade against them.

The goal was to explore how thoughtful UX and information design could make trading data more accessible and more useful to the trader.

The problem space

Trading data is only useful when it is easy to work with

Traders may need to review many sides of their activity, from a single trade to patterns across months. When that information is hard to organise, find or interpret, reviewing it takes extra effort. I framed the project around four themes.

01

Organising trading information

Grouping trades, notes and strategies into a clear structure instead of disconnected data points.

02

Understanding performance

Helping traders review their activity through clear summaries, readable tables and suitable charts.

03

Navigating complex information

Using navigation, filters, tables and page hierarchy to move between different views of the same data.

04

Supporting review and reflection

Making a deliberate review of past trades and trading days part of the routine.

Design opportunityCreate a desktop experience that brings trading information into a coherent structure and makes reviewing it more approachable.

These are the themes I designed around. They are not findings from a formal user study.

Key design decisions

Six decisions that shape Anchor

Each follows the same path: context, decision, rationale, the evidence in the interface, and the benefit I intended.

01 Overview02 Trade data03 Performance04 Strategy rules05 Trade review06 Navigation
01Creating a clear overview

The dashboard starts with what needs doing, then what happened

Context

A dashboard can show a lot of numbers without telling the trader where to start.

Decision

I put a review queue at the very top, followed by five headline figures, with deeper charts further down.

Rationale

Reviewing is the habit Anchor is built around, so the first thing on screen is the trader’s unfinished review work.

Intended benefit

Opening Anchor answers “what should I do now?” before “how am I doing?”

  • Each queue item is specific and actionable: “Review 12 trades”, “Tag 12 trades, needed for rule checks”, “Journal 3 sessions”. When everything is done, the queue changes to “You are all caught up”.
  • Each headline figure pairs a number with a simple visual (a gauge, a ring or a bar), plus a line of context such as “34 won · 73 lost”.
  • Every figure has a plain-language explanation. Profit factor, for example, reads “Gross profit divided by gross loss. Above 1 means winners paid for losers.”
02Presenting trading data clearly

A trade table built to be scanned row by row

Context

A trade has a lot of attributes: symbol, side, result, times, prices, quantity, P&L, return and strategy.

Decision

I designed one dense, consistent table with a summary strip above it and search across symbol, strategy and trade ID.

Rationale

On a desktop screen, a well-aligned table is the fastest way to compare many trades side by side.

Intended benefit

The trader can find a trade, or spot a bad run, without opening each one.

  • Columns follow the life of a trade: when and what (date, symbol), which way and how it ended (side, status), then the numbers (open → close, entry, exit, quantity, net P&L, net ROI) and finally the strategy.
  • Side and result read at a glance: LONG and SHORT have their own coloured markers, WIN and LOSS are badges, and P&L is green or red.
  • Numbers are right-aligned so they can be compared down a column. The table shows 15 trades a page with clear paging (“Showing 1–15 of 110”).
  • A review checkbox on every row and a running count (“110 trades · 0 reviewed”) tie the table back to the review habit.
03Making performance easier to review

Charts that explain themselves, and one score with its working shown

Context

Trading metrics such as profit factor, drawdown and R-multiples are easy to misread.

Decision

I matched each question to a chart type and attached a short explanation to every chart. I also designed a single Anchor Score that shows how it is calculated.

Rationale

A chart is only useful if the trader knows what it measures, so the explanation sits right next to the title.

Intended benefit

Numbers become something the trader can act on, not just something to look at.

  • Each chart answers one question: bars for daily P&L, a running line for cumulative P&L, an area for drawdown with its maximum labelled, and bubbles for trade time (placed at entry time, sized by position value).
  • Charts lead somewhere. Clicking a daily bar opens that day in the Journal.
  • The Anchor Score shows its working: it averages five parts, and hovering each point explains its formula (for example, “Green days ÷ trading days” for Consistency).
  • Reports turn patterns into plain sentences, such as “Winners are held 11m longer than losers” and “59 of 73 losers were in profit before closing at a loss”.
04Turning strategies into rules

Every trade is checked against the rules the trader set for themselves

Context

Anchor is for traders who “trade with intent”. Results alone don’t show whether a trader followed their own plan.

Decision

I made a strategy a set of checkable rules (time window, direction, trades per day, risk, reward to risk, stop loss) and checked every tagged trade against them.

Rationale

Discipline becomes visible and measurable, not just a feeling at the end of the day.

Intended benefit

The trader can see which rules they keep, which they break, and how often.

  • A guided five-step wizard (Basics, Context, Direction, Risk, Review) with inline validation. For example, it won’t accept more than 5% risk per trade: “Most traders risk 0.5% to 2%.”
  • Rule checks appear where the trader looks: on each trade (KEPT per rule), on the dashboard as a heatmap and a “Most broken rules” list, and per strategy as “Rules kept”.
  • Templates such as “Opening range breakout” give new traders a starting point, and every field can be changed.
  • “Block trade” is a proposed conceptSimulated: with supported brokers, Anchor would ask the broker to reject orders that break the strategy’s rules. The toggle is only a mock-up in the prototype.
05Supporting review and reflection

One page to review a trade, and a journal to review the day

Context

Reviewing a trade properly means seeing the numbers, the chart, the plan and your own notes together.

Decision

I designed a trade review page with the stats on the left and the chart and notes on the right, plus a Journal with one card per trading day.

Rationale

Keeping everything about one trade on one screen avoids jumping between tools.

Intended benefit

Review becomes quick enough to do after every session.

  • The chart shows the plan against what happened: entry, exit, stop and target on a candlestick chart (1m to 1h), a Running P&L view, and a Replay that plays the trade back.
  • Reflection is structured: star rating, tags such as “Followed plan” or “FOMO entry”, and note templates for a pre-trade checklist, a post-trade review and a mistake log.
  • Built for a routine: “Mark as reviewed”, then press n to jump to the next unreviewed trade. Days without a note are easy to find in the Journal.
06Navigation and useful interactions

A focused workspace that stays oriented as you dig in

Context

The same trades are viewed from five different angles, and a trader often wants to narrow them down.

Decision

I used a fixed sidebar of five areas, one shared set of filters and date range, breadcrumbs on deeper pages, and keyboard shortcuts.

Rationale

A filter set once should follow the trader, so every view shows the same slice of data.

Intended benefit

Less re-filtering and fewer clicks, with the trader always knowing where they are.

  • Five areas in a fixed sidebar, with the current one highlighted and the broker’s sync status (“Zerodha connected · Last synced…”) always visible at the bottom.
  • One filter set and date range (All time, This month, Last 30 or 90 days, or a custom range) apply across Dashboard, Trades, Journal and Reports.
  • Breadcrumbs such as “Trades / Trade review” and “Strategy / New strategy” lead back up.
  • Density with restraint: white cards on a light grey background, with figures given the most visual weight. The prototype also includes a dark theme.
The interface

Designing for clarity in a data-heavy environment

From Figma to a working prototype

Making the design experience tangible

Figma let me define Anchor’s structure, visual hierarchy and intended behaviour. I then used Claude to turn those designs into a working prototype, so the screens connect and the interactions can be tried rather than imagined.

Works in the prototype

  • Navigation between all five areas, breadcrumbs and keyboard shortcuts
  • Filters and date ranges that update every view
  • Trade search, paging, trade review tabs, chart timeframes, Running P&L and Replay
  • Tags, ratings, notes and note templates, mark as reviewed
  • Reports with switchable metrics and charts that link to the Journal
  • The five-step strategy wizard with validation, plus light and dark themes

Simulated or proposed

  • The broker connection and import are simulated, and run on a demo account of 110 sample trades
  • Block trade is a concept only; it doesn’t talk to any broker
  • Notes and changes are kept in the browser, not in an account
  • Real broker integrations, sync and data security would need full development

Reflection

Designing for understanding, not just information density

Anchor pushed me to think about how a desktop product can present a lot of numbers and still feel organised. The charts mattered less than the structure around them: a clear map of five areas, one way of filtering that follows you, and the same layout repeated across reports.

The most important decision was treating a strategy as a set of rules. It gave the product a point of view: Anchor is about whether you traded your plan, not just whether you made money. Building the prototype also let me test whether the screens hold together as one connected product rather than a set of static frames.

Outcome

A designed desktop experience and a working prototype that demonstrate the product concept. Anchor is a graduation project: it hasn’t launched, and I have no usability test results or measured outcomes to report.
