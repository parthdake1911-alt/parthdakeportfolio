One platform. Four roles. A more intuitive employee experience.

Project overview

Taking an existing website experience into a mobile app.

Tata Power wanted to bring its existing Customer Smile website to internal employees as a mobile app. The aim wasn’t to squeeze the website onto a smaller screen: the client wanted an experience that was more intuitive and easier to use, and wanted to explore gamification.

Customer Smile serves four groups with different responsibilities: employees, managers, administrators and jury members. I worked across the whole UX/UI process, from requirements and role-specific workflows to Figma wireframes, detailed UI and a working prototype built with Claude, staying in regular contact with the client and incorporating their feedback along the way.

The goal was a more coherent mobile experience that made the platform easier to use while addressing the needs of every role.

The design challenge

One app serving four different user journeys.

The core challenge was keeping the app consistent while supporting the different tasks each role needed to complete.

  1. 01

    Moving from website to mobile

    The experience had to be rethought for mobile, with navigation, screen space, readability and the order of interactions in mind, rather than reproduced on a smaller screen.

  2. 02

    Designing for four roles

    Employees, managers, administrators and jury members use the platform differently. Each needed a relevant experience without unnecessary complexity.

  3. 03

    Simplifying workflows

    The client wanted the process to be easier, which meant understanding each role’s steps and organising them more clearly.

  4. 04

    Introducing gamification thoughtfully

    Gamification had to support engagement without pulling people away from the task they came to do.

  5. 05

    Aligning with stakeholder feedback

    The experience evolved through regular client discussions, from early wireframes through UI design and prototyping.

Understanding the four user roles

Different responsibilities. Different workflows.

The prototype shown here is the employee app. The other three roles appear inside it wherever their work reaches the employee, so each one is described below through what the prototype actually shows.

01Interface in this prototype

Employee

Shares customer stories and best practices, appreciates colleagues’ stories and follows each entry through review.

  • Read and appreciate colleagues’ stories
  • Track each entry in My story status
  • Respond to change requests in the Bin

02Seen through the employee journey

Manager

Reviews and recommends entries from their team.

  • “Manager review” is a stage in every entry’s status
  • A manager comment can send a story back (“Your manager left a comment”)
  • The programme timeline has a Recommendation step: “Managers recommend entries from their teams”
03Seen through the employee journey

Administrator

Runs the programme and checks entries such as nominations.

  • Change requests signed “Admin team · Administrator”
  • Example: a nomination asked for one more line on customer impact
04Seen through the employee journey

Jury member

Evaluates entries for the quarter’s awards.

  • “Stories shared by 30 June go to this quarter’s jury”
  • Jury change requests, such as asking for a photo to verify a timeline
  • Jury winners shown in Champions and on Home

Key design decisions

Six decisions behind the experience.

01 Roles02 Mobile03 Gamification04 Task flow05 Consistency06 Collaboration
01
Design decision

Making every role visible to the employee

Context
Four groups shape an entry’s journey, but the employee who shares a story only ever sees their own screens.
Design decision
Show the employee exactly where an entry is and who it is with, and attribute every request to a named person and role.
Rationale
When the people reviewing your work are visible, the process feels less like a black box, and you know whose note you’re answering.
Prototype evidence
Status tracks each entry through Submitted → Manager review → Published. Bin cards name the requester and their role (Assessor, Jury, Administrator), the entry type, when it was asked and a reply-by date.
Intended benefit
Fewer “where is my story?” moments, and change requests that are clear about who needs what.
02
Design decision

Simplifying the mobile experience

Context
The website experience had to become more intuitive and easier to use on a phone.
Design decision
One primary action, Share a smile, always within reach; five destinations in a bottom bar; and a quick share in a bottom sheet next to the full form.
Rationale
On mobile, the most important action should never be more than a thumb away. A quick share covers the moment someone wants to say thanks, while the full form is there for a complete entry.
Prototype evidence
Share a smile appears in the Home hero, in the compact header once you scroll, and as a floating button. Tapping it asks “What would you like to share?” (a smile or a best practice). The quick share needs only who made it happen and what happened (20–280 characters).
Intended benefit
Less effort to share, and fewer reasons to put it off.

03
Design decision

Introducing gamification

Context
The client wanted to explore gamification as part of the employee experience.
Design decision
Tie rewards to the core action of sharing and recognising, and keep each mechanic small, optional and visible where it’s relevant.
Rationale
Gamification works best as feedback and recognition around the real task, not a separate game competing with it.
Prototype evidence
A daily spin (once a day) awards points, a badge, a 2× boost or a café treat. Sharing gives +10 smile points. A quarter progress bar counts down to the jury deadline. Recognition and Champions show Jury and People’s choice rankings by quarter.
Intended benefit
A reason to come back, and visible recognition for the people behind the stories. These are intended benefits, not measured results.
04
Design decision

Creating a clearer task flow

Context
Writing a good customer story is the hardest part of the process.
Design decision
Break the story into guided prompts, offer help with the writing, and let people speak instead of type.
Rationale
Prompts like “Customer needed help with…” and “The step I took was…” match how people naturally tell the story. Voice and AI drafting lower the barrier for field staff who aren’t at a desk.
Prototype evidence
The full form has a cover photo (photo or illustration), a story title and two prompts, each with Help me write and Speak instead. Voice can be added as text or attached as a voice note. Department, location, attachments and Save as draft follow, and Continue stays disabled until the required parts are filled.
Intended benefit
Fewer half-finished entries and clearer stories for reviewers.
05
Design decision

Keeping the app consistent

Context
Many screens and states could easily drift apart.
Design decision
A shared system: the same colours, header pattern, pill buttons, soft cards and bottom bar across every screen.
Rationale
Familiar patterns let people focus on the task, and a consistent system makes room for new screens without redesigning the app.
Prototype evidence
Blue-to-green gradients mark the hero areas (Home, Profile, Champions). Every pushed screen has the same back-arrow header. Primary actions use the same green-blue pill, and status chips share one colour language: green for live, blue for in review, red for changes.
Intended benefit
An app that feels like one product.

06
Design decision

Iterating through client collaboration

Context
Requirements and direction had to stay aligned with the client throughout.
Design decision
I stayed in regular contact with the client and brought their feedback into each stage, from wireframes to Figma UI to the working prototype.
Rationale
Reviewing early and often kept the design connected to the client’s requirements.
Intended benefit
A design that grew with the client’s input instead of being presented once at the end.

Reflection
Designing one app for different ways of working.

Customer Smile pushed me to think beyond individual screens to how a single product supports four kinds of users. Each role needed its own workflow, but the app still had to feel like one experience.

Balancing that took care with information architecture, task clarity, interaction design, and the line between usability and gamification. Working closely with the client throughout made the design stronger at every stage.
